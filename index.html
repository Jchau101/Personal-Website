I would rather rebuild a number than accept one.

I'm Jonathan Chau, a sophomore at Michigan studying economics. I spend most of my time turning messy information into a figure somebody has to act on: a market size, a club budget, a fundraising pipeline. Usually the figure I was handed does not survive contact with the underlying data.

Ann Arbor, Michigan · by way of Jericho, New York

I have been frugal for as long as I can remember. I still ask my parents what things cost, mostly so I can watch how a price moves across a year and work out whether a purchase was considered or impulsive. What bothered me was that neither option felt right: spending felt wasteful, and sitting on cash meant watching it quietly lose value. Markets were the answer to that problem, which is how I ended up interested in them at all.

I put about 60% of what I earned at my accounting internship this summer into a Roth IRA and a brokerage account. Buy and hold, nothing clever. What surprised me is what having real money in the market did to my attention. I read the WSJ markets section now, and I listen to Goldman's markets podcasts on the walk to class. Six months ago I could not have told you the difference between forward guidance and a reaction function. Now I know that Chair Warsh has declined to give either one, and I find the argument for it genuinely interesting. Two years ago I would not have believed I would enjoy reading the news.

Healthcare came from somewhere less tidy. I had a skin condition through most of middle and high school that I dealt with the only way I knew how, which was to research it obsessively: papers on the underlying causes, then making my own formulations out of ingredients I had read up on. I liked the research more than I expected to, and for a while I thought that meant dermatology. What stopped me was the path. I love projects and real work and I dislike exams, and another decade of school for the privilege of doing research I was already doing did not add up.

So I went looking for the version of that interest that lived in business, and joined the Michigan Healthcare Business Club my freshman year. MHBC is a Ross-affiliated organization of about fifty students working at the intersection of healthcare and business, where each semester you either take on a consulting project with a real client or build a startup against a healthcare problem. My first client-facing project taught me something I did not expect: consulting is advisory, and I wanted to be closer to the execution. That is what pushed me toward healthcare investment banking, which I am now weighing against capital markets.

One thing I was wrong about. I assumed finance was mostly money-hungry people with a convenient relationship to ethics. What I actually found were people who care about the work to a degree that is hard to fake, and who will do whatever the work requires because of it.

The work

Jul to Aug 2026 Remote, Poland

Private Equity Intern, Forturum

Forturum is a search fund building a roll-up in Central and Eastern Europe. I ran sector work for the investment memorandum and sourcing for a Polish physiotherapy roll-up. The number I inherited put the sector at EUR 80 to 200 million. I rebuilt it from the bottom up using verified local pricing and got to a defensible EUR 40 million core niche, then checked that against a scaled UK precedent to make sure the answer was not just smaller but right. Smaller was the useful answer, because it changes what a buy-and-build strategy in that sector can reasonably be.

74 owner-direct leads from 400+ companies screened against a buy box weighting revenue scale, EBITDA margin, city tier, owner age, and ownership structure. Python scrapers against the KRS court register turned hours of desk research per company into one command.

Jan 2026 to present Ann Arbor

Finance Chair and Consulting Analyst, Michigan Healthcare Business Club

I own the club's money: the budget, the sponsorship pipeline, the grant applications, and the part where somebody has to say what we cannot afford. Right now that means a 24-student trek to New York in November, sitting down with Trinity Life Sciences, Simon-Kucher, Kearney, and Zingage. The next section is that budget, live.

$19K in corporate sponsorships raised for competition prize pools. A weighted scoring model across 80+ biotech and pharma firms for a life-sciences recruiting client.

Jun to Aug 2026 New York

Accounting Intern, Miu & Company

Documentation for 40+ individual, S-corp, and partnership returns, bookkeeping, client P&Ls and balance sheets, and state filings. It is the least glamorous line here and probably the most useful. Reading a lot of small, real, badly organized financials is how you find out what the statements are actually made of.

Also the internship that funded the brokerage account, which is how the market stopped being theoretical.

Aug 2026 to present Ann Arbor

Business Development Associate, CreADE

An early-stage Michigan creatine-wellness startup that came out of MHBC's venture track. I pitch gym owners, retailers, and supplement companies on carrying us. Selling a physical product to a skeptical buyer is a fast way to find out which assumptions in a plan were load-bearing.

A 40+ partner pipeline, worked through in-person pitches and multi-touch follow-up designed to turn sampling into recurring orders.

Jan to Mar 2024 Melville, New York

Student Visionaries of the Year Candidate, Blood Cancer United

I led 24 people to raise $46,025 in seven weeks against a $20,000 goal. We started where everybody starts, with personal asks, and each person on the team found someone with a direct relationship to cancer. People were generous and the list ran out. I measured roughly 10% response on cold outreach against roughly 80% on warm introductions, moved the campaign onto referral sourcing, and then onto corporate grant and sponsorship programs where the ask was institutional rather than personal.

230% of target. 6 corporate sponsorships spanning a national rental brand and Long Island family businesses, delivering $25K+ of the total and making corporate giving the largest single source. Largest single gift $15,000 from Hertz.

Jun to Jul 2024 New York

Finance Intern, Jimmy Crystal New York

The summer I first opened Excel for something that mattered. I built the trackers the finance team used to monitor account activity and the revenue charts the manager took into client meetings. I was in high school and somebody was relying on my file being right, which is a specific kind of education.

Two years later I am still doing a version of the same thing, with better questions.

What I'm learning right now

The honest version is that I can find and defend a number, and I am still building the fluency that makes it fast. Four things this fall.

Technicals

I have been through accounting, enterprise versus equity value, DCF, merger models, and LBOs. I can recall them. I cannot always tell you why the mechanics work the way they do, and memorized is not the same as understood.

Markets

A daily read: Fed policy, yields, commodities, earnings, sector moves. I started close to zero on this, which is exactly why it is on the list rather than in the section above.

Investing

A Roth IRA I run myself, as a small live lab. Dividends and DRIP, how an ETF behaves differently from a single position, the difference between selling inside the account and taking money out of it. Real money makes the mechanics stick.

Open question

How much of a bottom-up model should be hard-coded and how much should be driven. I keep building things that are right once and painful to update.

Why Recalc

I found Recalc late, through Dylan Pinkins, and then did my own diligence on it. I watched the Fall 2026 info session, and what got me was the way Katie talked about the program. Most things aimed at students like me are recruiting help with a short shelf life. That is not how she described this one, and I believed her.

What I want from the Finance Accelerator is specific. Everything I have built so far lives in documents. Memos, decks, scoring rubrics, a grant application. They were good enough because nobody needed to open them and change an assumption. I have the technicals memorized and I do not have the intuition underneath them, and reading another PDF on my own is not going to fix that. Being in a live session where somebody watches me build and tells me where the model breaks is a much faster path.

What I would bring: I am the person who volunteers for the budget. I am also the person my friends call when something goes wrong, which has turned out to be a more useful skill than I expected in every team I have been on. I have taught myself enough to be useful and not enough to be comfortable, and that seems like a good place to be learning from.

The part that isn't on my resume

Investment banking was never supposed to be the destination. I want it because of what it teaches: the framework for looking at a business, the people, and the habit of forecasting well enough that a risk is visible before it arrives. What I actually want is to run something. Probably a roll-up.

My dad has been an entrepreneur for over thirty years and has owned several restaurants in New York. He never got to go to university. What he knew about running a restaurant was what his parents handed down to him, and he built a lot on it. I have since spent real time on restaurant margins and I understand now why the thing he was doing is so hard to scale.

I do not think he was missing intelligence or effort. He was missing a framework, and the framework is learnable. That is most of why I am doing any of this. Banking teaches you to ask the correct question rather than the smart-sounding one, and I would like to bring that home.

One other thing. I was admitted to Michigan and rejected from Ross. At the time it felt like the rest of college had been decided before it started, and it still lands a little when someone asks whether I am in Ross. Where I have ended up is that the program you get into sets a starting position and not much else. Everything on this page happened outside of it.
