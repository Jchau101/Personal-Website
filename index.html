Hi, I'm Jonathan.

I'm a sophomore at the University of Michigan studying economics, from Long Island, New York. I grew up around my dad's restaurants, asking a lot of questions about what things cost and why. That turned into an interest in business and markets that I am still in the early part of.

This page is what I have worked on, what I do when I am not working, and what I am still learning.

University of Michigan · BA Economics, expected May 2029

I have been careful with money for as long as I can remember. I still ask my parents what things cost, mostly so I can watch how a price moves over a year and work out whether buying it was considered or impulsive. What bothered me as a kid was that neither option felt right. Spending felt wasteful, and sitting on cash meant watching it quietly lose value. Markets were the answer to that, which is how I got interested in them at all.

I put about 60% of what I earned at my accounting internship this summer into a Roth IRA and a brokerage account. Buy and hold, nothing clever. What surprised me was what having my own money in the market did to my attention. I read the WSJ markets section now, and I listen to Goldman's markets podcasts on the walk to class. Six months ago I could not have told you the difference between forward guidance and a reaction function. I am still working out what I think about Chair Warsh declining to give either one, but I find the argument for it genuinely interesting. Two years ago I would not have believed I would enjoy reading the news.

Healthcare came from somewhere less tidy. I had a skin condition through most of middle and high school and I dealt with it the only way I knew how, which was to research it obsessively. Papers on what was actually causing it, then making my own formulations out of ingredients I had read up on. I liked the research more than I expected to, and for a while I thought that meant dermatology. What stopped me was the path rather than the subject. I like projects and real work and I dislike exams, and another decade of school to do research I was already doing did not add up.

So I went looking for the version of that interest that lived in business, and joined the Michigan Healthcare Business Club my freshman year. MHBC is about fifty students working somewhere between healthcare and business, and each semester you either take a consulting project with a real client or build a startup against a healthcare problem. My first project with a client taught me something I did not expect, which is that consulting is mostly advice, and I wanted to be closer to the part where something actually gets done. That is what pushed me toward healthcare investment banking, which I am now weighing against capital markets.

One thing I was wrong about. I assumed finance was mostly money-hungry people with a convenient relationship to ethics. What I found instead were people who care about the work more than I expected anyone to, and who will do whatever it takes because they care.

The work

Jul to Aug 2026 Remote, Poland

Private Equity Intern, Forturum

Forturum is a search fund buying and combining small healthcare businesses in Central and Eastern Europe. I worked on a Polish physiotherapy roll-up, doing the sector research behind the investment memo and finding companies worth approaching. The estimate I inherited put the sector somewhere between 80 and 200 million euros, which is a wide enough range that it does not really tell you anything. I rebuilt it from the ground up using real local pricing and got to about 40 million for the part that mattered, then checked that against a comparable UK market to make sure I had not just made the number smaller. Smaller turned out to be the useful answer, because it changes what you can realistically build there.

The hard part was not the analysis. We were a group of interns being given associate-level work, mostly first and second years, and when one person fell behind everyone downstream waited. Getting people on the same page was harder than any of the modeling.

I screened just over 400 Polish clinics and came back with 74 worth actually calling. The filter was mostly size, margin, what city they were in, and whether the owner was close enough to retirement to want to sell. I also wrote Python scripts to pull the registry filings automatically, which turned a few hours of research per company into one command.

Jan 2026 to present Ann Arbor

Finance Chair and Consulting Analyst, Michigan Healthcare Business Club

I look after the club's money. The budget, the sponsorship pipeline, the grant applications, and the part where somebody has to tell people what we cannot afford. Right now most of that is a trip to New York in November for 24 students, sitting down with Trinity Life Sciences, Simon-Kucher, Kearney, and Zingage. The next section is that budget, live, if you want to poke at it.

I have raised $19K in sponsorships, which funds the prize pools for the club's pitch and case competitions. I also built a scoring model that worked through 80-plus biotech and pharma companies for a life sciences recruiting client, to figure out which ones were worth their time.

Jun to Aug 2026 New York

Accounting Intern, Miu & Company

Documentation for 40-plus individual, S-corp, and partnership returns, plus bookkeeping, client P&Ls and balance sheets, and state filings. It is the least exciting thing on this page and probably the most useful. Reading a lot of small, real, badly organized financials is how you find out what the statements are actually made of.

It is also the job that funded the brokerage account, which is how the market stopped being theoretical for me.

Aug 2026 to present Ann Arbor

Business Development Associate, CreADE

An early-stage creatine wellness startup that came out of MHBC's venture track. I pitch gym owners, retailers, and supplement companies on stocking us. Selling a physical product to someone who has no particular reason to say yes is a quick way to find out which parts of a plan were doing real work and which were decoration.

A pipeline of 40-plus possible partners, worked through in-person pitches and a lot of follow-up, trying to turn people who sampled it into people who reorder it.

Jan to Mar 2024 Melville, New York

Student Visionaries of the Year Candidate, Blood Cancer United

I led a team of 24 people raising money for blood cancer research, with a $20,000 goal and seven weeks to do it. We started the way everyone starts, with personal asks, and each of us found someone with a real connection to cancer. People were generous, and then the list ran out. So I started tracking what was actually working and found we were getting around 10% response on cold outreach against around 80% on warm introductions. We stopped cold outreach almost entirely, moved onto referrals, and then onto corporate grant programs where the ask was not personal at all.

We finished at $46,025, a little over twice the goal. Six corporate sponsorships made up more than $25K of that, and the largest single gift was $15,000 from Hertz.

Jun to Jul 2024 New York

Finance Intern, Jimmy Crystal New York

The summer I first opened Excel for something that mattered. I built the trackers the finance team used to watch account activity, and the revenue charts the manager brought into client meetings. I was still in high school and somebody was relying on my file being right, which teaches you something a class cannot.

Two years later I am still doing a version of the same thing, with better questions.

What I'm learning right now

The honest version is that I can find a number and defend it, and I am still building the fluency that makes it quick. Four things this fall.

Technicals

I have been through accounting, enterprise versus equity value, DCF, merger models, and LBOs. I can recall them. I cannot always tell you why the mechanics work the way they do, and memorized is not the same as understood.

Markets

A daily read: Fed policy, yields, commodities, earnings, sector moves. I started close to zero on this, which is exactly why it is on this list and not the one above.

Investing

A Roth IRA I run myself, which is a small and fairly slow lab. Dividends and DRIP, how an ETF behaves differently from a single position, the difference between selling inside the account and taking money out of it. Real money makes the mechanics stick.

Open question

How much of a bottom-up model should be hard-coded and how much should be driven off inputs. I keep building things that are right once and then painful to update, and I do not yet know where the line is.

Why Recalc

I found Recalc late, through Dylan Pinkins, and then went and looked into it myself. I watched the Fall 2026 info session, and what got me was the way Katie talked about the program. Most things aimed at students like me are recruiting help with a short shelf life. That is not how she described this one, and I believed her.

What I want from the Finance Accelerator is pretty specific. Everything I have built so far lives in documents. Memos, decks, scoring rubrics, a grant application. They were good enough because nobody needed to open them and change an assumption. I have the technicals memorized without the intuition underneath them, and reading another PDF on my own is not going to fix that. Sitting in a live session where somebody watches me build and tells me where the model breaks is a much faster way to learn it.

As for what I would bring: I am the person who volunteers for the budget. I am also the person my friends call when something goes wrong, which has turned out to matter more in a team than I expected it to. I have taught myself enough to be useful and not enough to be comfortable, which seems like a reasonable place to be learning from.

The part that isn't on my resume

Investment banking was never supposed to be the destination. I want it for what it teaches: a framework for looking at a business, the people, and the habit of forecasting well enough that you can see a risk before it arrives. What I actually want, eventually, is to run something. Probably a roll-up.

My dad has been an entrepreneur for over thirty years and has owned several restaurants in New York. He never got to go to university. What he knew about running a restaurant was what his parents handed down to him, and he built a lot on that. I have since spent real time on restaurant margins and I understand now why what he was doing is so hard to scale.

I do not think he was missing intelligence or effort. He was missing a framework, and a framework is a learnable thing. That is most of why I am doing any of this. Banking teaches you to ask the correct question instead of the smart-sounding one, and I would like to bring that home.

One other thing. I was admitted to Michigan and rejected from Ross. At the time it felt like the rest of college had been decided before it started, and it still lands a little when someone asks whether I am in Ross. Where I have ended up is that the program you get into sets a starting position and not much else. Everything on this page happened outside of it.
